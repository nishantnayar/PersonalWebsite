<p class="lede">I have two degrees that don't usually show up together: an MBA in Finance and a Master's in Analytics from UChicago. Most people hear that and ask me which one I actually am.</p>

<p>I stopped trying to answer that question about ten years ago.</p>

<p>The reaction I get when I mention both degrees is pretty consistent. People pause, look slightly puzzled, and ask: <em>"So… are you a business person or a data person?"</em> It's a fair question. We're trained to put people in boxes. Quant or strategist. Left brain or right brain. The spreadsheet guy or the PowerPoint guy.</p>

<p>But that framing is exactly what creates the problem I've spent my career trying to solve.</p>

<h2>The Same Scene, Every Organization</h2>

<p>I've worked across financial services for over a decade — investment banking, asset management, commercial banking. Different institutions, different asset classes, different leadership styles. One thing never changes.</p>

<p>The gap isn't technical capability. The data team knows their models. The business side knows their market. The gap is translation — and translation requires someone who's been fluent in both rooms.</p>

<blockquote>
  <p>"Rigor without trust doesn't change anything. Speed without rigor doesn't last. The valuable thing is knowing how to build both, together."</p>
</blockquote>

<h3>Why it keeps happening</h3>

<p>Finance organizations hire for depth. You're a credit analyst, a quant, a portfolio manager, a risk officer. That specialization makes sense — these are hard jobs that require genuine expertise. But it creates org charts where no one's formal role is to hold the two worlds together.</p>

<p>The result: analytical work gets built in isolation from the decisions it's supposed to inform. Business decisions get made without the analytical scaffolding that would make them defensible. And the chasm widens with every quarter.</p>

<h2>What "Sitting in Both Rooms" Actually Taught Me</h2>

<p>My roles across the years — Solutions Analyst, Lead Technical Program Manager — have all been variations on the same job description, even when that wasn't written anywhere: figure out what each side actually needs, and build the bridge between them.</p>

<p>A few things I've learned doing that work:</p>

<h3>Business people don't distrust data — they distrust models they can't interrogate</h3>
<p>When a senior leader pushes back on an analytical recommendation, it's usually not because they don't believe in data. It's because they can't tell where the assumptions are buried, and they've been burned before. The fix isn't better models. It's more transparent ones — with clearer documentation of what inputs drive which outputs, and explicit acknowledgment of where the model breaks down.</p>

<h3>Data people underestimate how much framing shapes adoption</h3>
<p>A technically correct analysis that lands in the wrong format, at the wrong meeting, with the wrong framing, is an analysis that doesn't get used. How you present findings — the sequence, the vocabulary, what you lead with — changes what decisions get made. That's not spin. It's communication.</p>

<h3>The translation layer has to be built into the process, not bolted on after</h3>
<p>The organizations I've seen get this right don't just bring in a translator at the end. They structure projects from the start with someone who can hold the business question and the analytical method in tension simultaneously — forcing both sides to define what "success" looks like before anyone writes a line of code or pulls a dataset.</p>

<div class="divider">· · ·</div>

<h2>Why I'm Writing About This Now</h2>

<p>The tools are changing fast. Better data infrastructure, machine learning in more workflows, AI starting to touch parts of financial services that seemed untouchable a few years ago. All of that makes the translation problem more urgent, not less.</p>

<p>More analytical horsepower doesn't close the gap automatically. It can widen it — if the business side can't evaluate what it's being handed, and the technical side doesn't understand what decisions actually need to be made.</p>

<p>The skillset that bridges that gap — financial intuition, analytical fluency, the ability to move between strategy and implementation — is the thing I've been building my entire career, sometimes intentionally and sometimes just by necessity.</p>

<p>I'm going to start writing about what that actually looks like in practice. Not theory. Specific problems, specific patterns, what works and what doesn't.</p>
